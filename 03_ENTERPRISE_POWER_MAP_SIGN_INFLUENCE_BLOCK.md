# Enterprise Power Map: Who Signs, Influences, Blocks, Wins, and Loses

**Purpose:** Map the real human power structure behind enterprise software purchasing and adoption.

---

## Executive summary

Enterprise decisions are negotiated across overlapping hierarchies:
- formal authority (who can sign),
- economic authority (who controls budget),
- operational authority (who absorbs change),
- reputational authority (who bears failure).

A deal closes when these authorities are aligned enough that action is safer than inaction.

---

## 1) Who signs?

Typical formal signers (varies by company size/governance):
- CFO (budget owner / financial authority)
- CIO or CTO (technology governance authority)
- COO (operational authority)
- Business unit president/GM (P&L owner)
- Procurement + legal final authorizers
- In some cases CEO signs above threshold or politically sensitive deals

**Reality:** Signature authority may be formal, but signers rarely originate demand. They ratify coalition consensus.

---

## 2) Who influences?

### Core influencers
- CEO chief of staff / strategy office (agenda shaping)
- CFO team / FP&A (economic framing)
- Enterprise architecture / security (risk credibility)
- Transformation office / PMO (delivery confidence)
- Data/AI governance leaders (policy legitimacy)
- External consultants (comparative legitimacy)
- Trusted peer CEOs/CFOs (social proof)

### Influence mechanics
Influence comes from reducing uncertainty for others:
- “This is benchmarked.”
- “This is governable.”
- “This won’t explode operationally.”
- “This can be explained to the board.”

---

## 3) Who blocks?

### Explicit blockers
- Security, privacy, risk, compliance, legal (formal veto points)
- Procurement (commercial terms / vendor risk concerns)

### Implicit blockers
- Middle managers losing informational control
- Functional leaders facing KPI exposure
- Teams with implementation overload
- Executives with competing transformation agendas

### Common blocking arguments (surface) vs motivations (latent)
- Surface: “not mature enough,” “integration risk,” “timing issue.”
- Latent: “loss of discretion,” “loss of narrative control,” “career downside.”

---

## 4) Who benefits?

### Primary beneficiaries
- CEO/CFO/COO when enterprise performance coherence improves
- P&L owners when cross-functional friction declines
- High-performing operators whose effectiveness becomes visible
- Governance teams when decision traceability improves

### Secondary beneficiaries
- Transformation leaders if successful rollout strengthens internal mandate
- Data/AI governance teams via clearer accountability standards

---

## 5) Who loses power?

Most likely power losers:
- actors who monetize ambiguity,
- gatekeepers of siloed information,
- managers relying on narrative control over measurable outcomes,
- groups whose influence came from being the only interpreters of fragmented data.

Power loss is typically about **loss of discretion**, not job loss.

---

## 6) Who fears transparency?

High transparency anxiety groups often include:
- owners of underperforming workflows,
- stakeholders with weak attribution trails,
- units whose KPIs improve locally while harming enterprise outcomes,
- leaders accustomed to consensus-by-ambiguity.

Transparency fear increases when incentives are not redesigned alongside visibility.

---

## 7) Who fears AI?

AI fear is heterogeneous:
- **Professional risk fear:** “My judgment authority is being replaced.”
- **Accountability fear:** “AI exposes decisions I can no longer justify informally.”
- **Control fear:** “I cannot explain or govern this reliably.”
- **Political fear:** “This shifts prestige from domain tenure to system fluency.”

Most enterprise AI resistance is governance and status anxiety, not anti-technology ideology.

---

## 8) Who gains promotion?

Likely promotion winners when rollout succeeds:
- executive sponsor who links software to hard outcomes,
- program leader who delivers with low disruption,
- functional leaders who adopt early and show measurable gains,
- governance leaders who create scalable control frameworks.

Promotion follows visible enterprise impact plus perceived leadership under uncertainty.

---

## 9) Who loses bonus?

Likely bonus pressure points:
- leaders tied to efficiency/quality metrics exposed by new transparency,
- managers unable to adapt workflows to accountability standards,
- sponsors who overpromised and under-delivered value realization.

Compensation risk is one of the strongest hidden drivers of resistance.

---

## 10) Who becomes accountable?

Enterprise accountability shifts from collective abstraction to named ownership:
- decision owner,
- economic owner,
- implementation owner,
- risk owner,
- outcome verifier.

Systems that make this explicit improve performance but intensify political friction in the short run.

---

## 12) Decision reality model

A purchase happens when all three inequalities hold:

1. **Perceived inaction risk > perceived adoption risk**  
2. **Coalition gain > coalition threat**  
3. **Sponsor survivability > sponsor exposure**

If any one fails, the organization delays, pilots indefinitely, or chooses incumbent safety.

---

## Final statement

Who signs the contract is not the same as who makes the decision.  
Enterprise adoption is a negotiated redistribution of power, accountability, and narrative control.  
The winning strategy is to design the deal so that enough powerful actors become safer by saying yes.
